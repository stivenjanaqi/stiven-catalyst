The KPI is not the problem. The process behind it is.

A bad number creates urgency. But urgency without diagnosis often produces pressure instead of improvement.

Stiven Janaqi
20 September 2026
7 min read

Managers are trained to watch numbers. Delays, damage, conversion, loading time, guest satisfaction, labour cost, quality, productivity : the language changes by industry, but the pattern is familiar.

When a number turns red, attention increases. Meetings get sharper. Messages become more frequent. Teams hear that the KPI has to improve.

Sometimes that pressure works for a day. It rarely fixes the system.

The number is an outcome

A KPI is usually the final visible result of many earlier decisions: planning, training, capacity, layout, handoffs, tools, staffing, communication and standards.

If the process stays the same, asking for a different number is mostly asking people to compensate harder.

That is why strong operational management starts one level deeper. Instead of asking only, “Why is the KPI bad?”, ask, “What sequence of events reliably creates this result?”

The useful question is not who touched the problem last. It is where the process first became unstable.

Move from pressure to evidence

Look at the work where it happens. Follow the handoff. Compare the expected process with the real one. Separate one-off mistakes from repeated patterns. Then decide whether the cause is skill, capacity, clarity, design or ownership.

This changes the management conversation. People stop defending themselves against a number and start contributing evidence about the process.

Better systems make better behaviour easier

The goal is not to remove accountability. It is to make accountability useful. People should own their decisions, but managers should also own the systems that shape those decisions.

A good KPI tells you where to look. It should not tell you who to blame.
